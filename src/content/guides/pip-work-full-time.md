One of the most persistent myths about Personal Independence Payment is that you cannot claim it if you work full time — or that holding down a job somehow proves you are not disabled enough to qualify. Neither of these things is true. PIP is one of the few major UK benefits that is completely unaffected by employment status, hours worked, or income. There is no earnings limit, no hours threshold, and no means test anywhere in the assessment.

Every year, thousands of people who work full time do not claim PIP because they have absorbed this myth, or because they talk themselves out of describing their real difficulties out of guilt about holding down a job while struggling at home. Both reactions cost people money they are legally entitled to.

PIP exists to help with the extra costs of long-term ill health or disability, regardless of whether you are in work. The question the Department for Work and Pensions is trying to answer is not "can this person hold a job?" but "how does this person's condition affect their ability to carry out everyday activities and get around, on the majority of days?" Those are completely different questions, and conflating them is the single biggest reason working claimants under-claim or get turned down unfairly.

This guide explains how PIP descriptors work, how to describe the reality of working full time with a health condition, what assessors are and are not allowed to hold against you, and how to avoid the mistakes that cost working claimants points they are entitled to.

## The myth that refuses to die

Ask around and you will hear versions of the same claim: "you can't get PIP if you work," "they'll say you can't be that disabled if you're holding down a job." None of this reflects how PIP law actually works.

The PIP assessment criteria make no reference to employment at all. The 12 activities that make up the Daily Living and Mobility components — preparing food, washing and bathing, dressing, communicating, managing therapy, budgeting, planning journeys, and moving around, among others — are assessed entirely on functional ability, not occupational status. Someone who works 40 hours a week through significant pain, exhaustion, medication, or workplace adjustments can be just as entitled to PIP, and sometimes more entitled, than someone who does not work at all.

Many working claimants have a very clear story to tell: they pour everything they have into getting through their working day, and there is little or nothing left for cooking, washing, dressing, socialising, or simply functioning once they get home. That pattern is not a reason to avoid claiming — it is often the central evidence of a strong claim.

## How PIP activities and descriptors work

PIP assessors score you against 12 activities split across two components. Daily Living covers ten activities and Mobility covers two. For each activity you are placed on the descriptor that best reflects your ability on the **majority of days** — meaning more than half the days in a rolling 12-month period, looking both backwards and forwards from your assessment.

Crucially, the test is not simply "can you physically do this once." The legal test asks whether you can complete an activity **safely, to an acceptable standard, repeatedly, and in a reasonable time** (no more than twice as long as someone without your condition would take). If you can only manage a task by using an aid, needing prompting, needing supervision, or needing another person's help, you score points even if you can technically complete it unaided on a good day.

This matters enormously for working claimants, because managing at work often depends on masking difficulty, using workplace equipment, taking on lighter duties, or drawing on reserves of energy and willpower that are completely spent by the time you get home. The descriptors below show exactly how points are awarded for some of the most commonly affected activities.

### Preparing food

- **Needs to use an aid or appliance to be able to either prepare or cook a simple meal — 2 points.** Perching stools, jar openers, one-handed equipment, or lightweight pans.
- **Needs prompting to be able to either prepare or cook a simple meal — 2 points.** You need reminding to start or finish cooking because of fatigue, pain, or cognitive symptoms.
- **Needs supervision or assistance to either prepare or cook a simple meal — 4 points.** Someone stays nearby because of safety risk, or physically helps with chopping, lifting pans, or draining water.
- **Cannot prepare and cook a simple meal — 8 points.** On the majority of days, cooking from scratch is not realistic even with aids, and someone else prepares meals or you rely on ready meals.

Many full-time workers can just about manage a microwave meal on work nights but cannot safely cook from scratch. That still counts.

### Managing therapy or monitoring a health condition

- **Needs aid or appliance — 1 point.** Pill organisers, alarms, medication reminder apps.
- **Needs supervision, prompting or assistance for less than 3.5 hours a week — 1 point.**
- **Needs supervision, prompting or assistance for 3.5 to 7 hours a week — 2 points.**
- **Needs supervision, prompting or assistance for 7 to 14 hours a week — 4 points.**
- **Needs supervision, prompting or assistance for 14 or more hours a week — 8 points.**

Add up medication management, physiotherapy exercises, and any prompting a partner provides across the whole week, not just work days.

### Washing and bathing

- **Needs to use an aid or appliance — 2 points.** Shower chair, grab rails, long-handled sponge.
- **Needs supervision or prompting — 2 points.** Fatigue or pain means you need encouragement to start, or someone nearby because of falls risk.
- **Needs assistance to wash between shoulders and waist — 2 points.**
- **Needs assistance to wash below waist — 4 points.**
- **Needs assistance to get in or out of the bath or shower — 3 points.**
- **Cannot wash and bathe at all — 8 points.**

If washing before work uses so much energy that you need to sit afterwards, or you skip washing some days to conserve energy, describe that clearly.

### Dressing and undressing

- **Needs to use an aid or appliance — 2 points.** Button hooks, sock aids, adapted fastenings.
- **Needs prompting to dress or undress, or to select appropriate clothing — 2 points.**
- **Needs assistance to dress or undress their lower body — 2 points.**
- **Needs assistance to dress or undress their upper body — 4 points.**
- **Cannot dress or undress at all — 8 points.**

If dressing for work takes far longer than it should, or must be planned around pain and stiffness first thing, that is exactly the detail assessors need.

### Engaging with other people face to face

- **Needs prompting to be able to engage with other people — 2 points.** You need encouragement to attend appointments, answer the door, or take part in conversations outside structured, familiar settings.
- **Needs social support to be able to engage with other people — 4 points.** You manage better with a familiar person present who can support communication or provide reassurance.
- **Cannot engage with other people due to such engagement causing overwhelming psychological distress to the claimant — 8 points.**

Workplace engagement is often scaffolded by routine and familiar colleagues. Engaging with new people or in unfamiliar settings can be a completely different, harder picture — and that difference is what matters for PIP.

### Making budgeting decisions

- **Needs prompting or assistance for complex budgeting decisions — 2 points.**
- **Needs prompting or assistance for simple budgeting decisions — 4 points.**
- **Cannot make any budgeting decisions at all — 6 points.**

If your salary is managed by a partner because fatigue or cognitive symptoms make bills unmanageable outside work, this activity is relevant even though you are employed and earning.

### Planning and following journeys

- **Needs prompting to be able to undertake any journey to avoid overwhelming psychological distress to the claimant — 4 points.**
- **Cannot plan the route of a journey — 8 points.**
- **Cannot follow the route of an unfamiliar journey without another person, an assistance dog or an orientation aid — 10 points.**
- **Cannot undertake any journey because it would cause overwhelming psychological distress to the claimant — 10 points.**
- **Cannot follow the route of a familiar journey without another person, an assistance dog or an orientation aid — 12 points.**

Commuting on a fixed, learned route is not the same as navigating anywhere unfamiliar. Many working claimants can only manage their commute because it is completely routine, and struggle badly with any journey that is new.

### Moving around

- **Can stand and then move more than 50 metres but no more than 200 metres, either aided or unaided — 4 points.**
- **Can stand and then move unaided more than 20 metres but no more than 50 metres — 8 points.**
- **Can stand and then move using an aid or appliance more than 20 metres but no more than 50 metres — 8 points** under the checker wording.
- **Can stand and then move more than 1 metre but no more than 20 metres, either aided or unaided — 10 points.**
- **Cannot, either aided or unaided, stand and then move more than 1 metre — 12 points.**

A short walk from a car park to a desk, supported by a lift or parking bay, does not reflect what happens on a bad day or outside work. Describe your reliable walking distance, not your best-case scenario.

## The majority of days rule — and why work makes it easy to understate your claim

PIP looks at the **majority of days**, not your single best day and not your performance during the hours you are propped up by routine and workplace support. This is exactly where working claimants tend to under-claim. If you can hold things together at your desk from 9 to 5, it is tempting to think that proves you manage fine — but PIP is interested in your whole day, including mornings, evenings, weekends, and days you take off sick.

A common, entirely legitimate pattern looks like this: get up in significant pain or exhaustion, spend a long time getting washed and dressed, use most of your remaining energy getting through the working day, arrive home unable to cook or engage socially, and spend the evening recovering so you can do it all again tomorrow. That is not evidence you do not have a disability — it is precisely the evidence PIP needs.

### Sample diary template for working claimants

Keep a diary for two to four weeks covering both work days and days off, so DWP can see the whole pattern rather than one snapshot.

| Date | Work day? | Morning routine | Symptoms at work | Evening capacity | Help needed | Recovery time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mon | Yes | 45 min to wash/dress, needed to sit twice | Pain 7/10, used standing desk, colleague carried files | Too exhausted to cook, ordered food | None at work, partner cooked at home | Went to bed at 8pm |
| Tue | Yes | Skipped washing hair to save time and energy | Struggled to concentrate after lunch, extra breaks | Could not manage stairs at home, used stairlift | Partner helped with dinner and dishes | Whole evening lost |
| Sat | No | Slower but no time pressure | N/A | Managed a short walk and some chores | Occasional help from partner | Better, but still tired by evening |

Weekly summary example: "On four of five working days I needed help washing my hair or dressing my lower body because of pain and fatigue. On the majority of days, after work, I cannot cook a meal from scratch and rely on my partner or ready meals. I need a full rest day most weekends simply to recover from the working week."

## What rate you might expect

There is no automatic rate for working claimants — the award depends entirely on your functional needs, exactly as it would for anyone else. Working full time neither boosts nor reduces your chances; it is simply irrelevant to the scoring.

For 2026/27, the weekly rates are:

- **Daily Living:** Standard rate £72.65, Enhanced rate £108.55
- **Mobility:** Standard rate £28.70, Enhanced rate £75.75

In broad terms:

- people who need occasional aids, some prompting, and manage tasks with extra time or effort often reach the **Standard** rate on one or both components
- people who need regular supervision or physical assistance, cannot manage journeys without support, or have limited reliable walking distance often reach the **Enhanced** rate
- small difficulties across several activities can add up to a substantial award even where no single activity looks dramatic alone

Be realistic and consistent. Describe your functional reality, not your single best day, and not a worse picture than you actually experience.

## Claiming while working — demolishing the myths

This is where more full-time workers trip up than anywhere else, so it is worth taking apart each myth individually.

**Myth: "If I can work, I can't really be disabled."** Disability and employability are not the same thing. People manage to work with severe pain, fatigue, mental health conditions, and progressive illnesses every day, often at significant personal cost. PIP is explicitly about the extra costs of disability, not capacity for work, which is assessed separately under Universal Credit's Work Capability Assessment.

**Myth: "Working full time will be held against me at assessment."** Assessors are trained not to treat employment as evidence against significant functional limitations. The ability to work says nothing on its own about ability to complete the 12 PIP activities safely, repeatedly, to an acceptable standard, and in a reasonable time. If a decision maker uses your job as a blanket reason to disallow points without engaging with your functional evidence, that is a legal error that can be challenged at mandatory reconsideration.

**Myth: "I should downplay my claim because it feels wrong to claim while working."** PIP is not means-tested and is not an out-of-work benefit. It exists to offset the extra costs disabled people face, including those who work. Claiming while employed is exactly what the benefit is designed for.

**Workplace adaptations vs home difficulties.** This is the most important distinction to explain on your form. Many workplaces provide adjustments — flexible hours, an accessible desk, reduced physical duties, extra breaks, or understanding colleagues — that make work manageable in a way home life simply is not. If your employer provides an ergonomic chair and a lift, but your home has stairs and no aids, your difficulties at home may be far more severe than anything visible at work. Describe the adaptations that make work possible, then describe what happens without them.

**Cumulative fatigue after work.** Many conditions — chronic pain, fibromyalgia, ME/CFS, mental health conditions, neurological conditions — involve a finite daily reserve of energy. Spending that reserve at work means little or nothing is left for the rest of the day, sometimes called payback or post-exertional exhaustion. This is directly relevant to how you manage cooking, washing, and engaging with people once home. "After a full shift I am too exhausted to cook and go straight to bed" is far more useful than "work is tiring."

**Assessors cannot use employment against you.** Your job title, hours, and salary are not PIP descriptors. No rule in PIP legislation reduces entitlement because you are employed. If your award is refused and employment is cited without proper engagement with your functional evidence, that is grounds for mandatory reconsideration.

## What to write on your PIP form — specific do/don't examples

Assessors need concrete, specific, functional detail, not general statements about having a condition or a job.

**Don't write:** "I work full time but it's hard."
**Do write:** "I work full time using a supportive chair, reduced physical duties, and regular breaks arranged by my employer. Without these adjustments I could not manage a working day at all, and even with them I am unable to cook, wash again, or leave the house on the majority of evenings afterwards."

**Don't write:** "I struggle a bit with tiredness."
**Do write:** "On the majority of working days I am too exhausted after my shift to prepare a meal, so I rely on ready meals or my partner cooking. I need to go to bed within an hour of getting home on at least four out of five working days."

**Don't write:** "I manage okay most of the time."
**Do write:** "I can only manage my commute because it is a completely familiar, unchanging route. I cannot reliably plan or follow an unfamiliar journey without my partner, because anxiety and disorientation make this unsafe."

**Don't write:** "I have a bit of help with money."
**Do write:** "My partner manages our household budgeting and bills because concentration and fatigue after work mean I cannot reliably process paperwork or make financial decisions safely."

## What evidence helps

Strong evidence for a working claimant focuses on function, not just diagnosis or job title:

- GP letters describing your condition's functional impact, not just its name
- specialist or consultant letters, particularly any that mention fatigue, pain levels, or restrictions
- occupational health reports or workplace adjustment records
- physiotherapy, occupational therapy, or mental health professional letters
- a completed symptom or activity diary covering both work days and rest days
- statements from a partner, family member, or friend describing what they see at home
- medication lists, especially anything relevant to fatigue, pain relief, or side effects

Occupational health reports and workplace adjustment agreements are some of the strongest evidence you can provide, since they independently confirm that significant adaptations are needed just to keep you in work.

## Working with your medical team

Ask your GP or specialist to write about function rather than diagnosis alone. Useful areas to cover include how your condition affects stamina, pain, or concentration across a full day, any restrictions or adjustments recommended for work, how symptoms are likely to affect you outside structured environments, and medication side effects that affect alertness or energy.

Bring your diary to appointments. A clinician who can see a written weekly pattern, including what happens after work, is far better placed to describe your real functional picture than one working from a single short consultation.

## What happens at the assessment — misconceptions assessors have about employment

Assessors sometimes assume that because someone arrived smartly dressed, spoke clearly, and mentioned a full-time job, they must be coping well overall. It is important to actively correct this impression rather than letting it pass unchallenged.

### Before the assessment

- write down the specific workplace adjustments you rely on, so you can describe them precisely
- note how you typically feel in the hours and days after work, not just during it
- bring your diary, medication list, and any occupational health paperwork

### During the assessment

- state clearly and early that you are only able to work because of specific adaptations, and name them
- explain what happens without those adaptations — evenings, weekends, days off
- do not let "I work full time" stand alone as a sentence; always follow it with the support and cost involved
- if you push through pain or exhaustion to get to work, say so directly rather than presenting a falsely capable picture

If an assessor implies that your job disproves your claim, you are entitled to challenge that, including later through mandatory reconsideration. Employment status is not a valid basis for reducing your score.

## Common mistakes

**Not claiming at all because of the "you can't get PIP if you work" myth.**
This single misconception stops many eligible working people from ever applying.

**Describing only the working day, not the recovery afterwards.**
The evening, weekend, and cumulative fatigue picture is often the strongest part of a working claimant's case.

**Failing to mention workplace adaptations explicitly.**
If adjustments are what make work possible, say so directly — otherwise the assessor may assume you manage everything unaided.

**Downplaying difficulties out of guilt about claiming while employed.**
PIP is not means-tested and is not reserved for people who cannot work. Minimising your evidence only reduces your own award.

**Answering based on a good day or a light week at work.**
Majority-of-days means your typical pattern, including bad days and flare-ups.

## PIP reviews and reassessments

Awards are usually reviewed periodically, and your circumstances — including your job — may well have changed by then. Employment status still has no bearing on the review outcome, but your functional needs might have shifted, for better or worse.

At review:

- describe your current situation honestly, including any change in job, hours, or workplace adjustments
- if your condition has progressed and work now costs you more than at the last assessment, say so clearly
- keep updated medical evidence, occupational health records, and a fresh diary if your pattern has changed

Do not assume DWP will infer anything from the fact you are still working. Spell out, again, exactly what that costs you functionally.

## FAQ

### Can I claim PIP if I work full time?

Yes. PIP has no earnings limit, no hours limit, and no means test. Employment status has no bearing on entitlement.

### Will working count against me at my assessment?

It should not. Assessors are trained not to treat employment as evidence against significant functional limitations. If it is used that way, you can challenge this at mandatory reconsideration.

### Does PIP affect my wages or tax?

No. PIP is paid separately, is not means-tested, and is not taxable.

### What if I can only work because of workplace adjustments?

Explain this clearly. Adjustments that make work possible are strong evidence of significant functional limitations, not evidence against your claim.

### I'm exhausted after work but fine at my desk — does that count?

Yes. PIP looks at the majority of days across your whole life, not just the hours you are propped up by routine at work. Cumulative fatigue after work is directly relevant.

### Should I mention my job on the PIP form?

Yes, but always pair it with the adjustments you need and what happens outside those supported hours.

### Can claiming PIP affect my employer's view of me?

No. PIP is a personal, non-means-tested benefit unrelated to your employer or your capacity for work as assessed under separate benefits.
